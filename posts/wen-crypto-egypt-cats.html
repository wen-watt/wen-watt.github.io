Cats in ancient Egypt: the facts

  • Guardians of food. Cats protected granaries and homes from rodents and snakes — in a land that lived from its harvest, that was survival, not decoration.
  • Bastet. The goddess Bastet, shown as a cat or a woman with a cat's head, stood for protection, the home, joy and fertility. Her cult centre was the city of Bubastis in the Nile Delta.
  • Protected by law and custom. Greek visitors such as Herodotus and Diodorus wrote that killing a cat in Egypt could bring the harshest punishment, even by accident.
  • Remembered forever. Huge numbers of cats were mummified and buried as offerings; whole cemeteries of them have been found by archaeologists.

The tale of Amset the scribe

In the golden light of Thebes, where the Nile mirrored the stars, a young scribe named Amset received a task from the high priest: to write down the sacred meaning of the animals that lived among the people. Of all of them, the cat fascinated him most.

At dawn he sat in the shade of Bastet's temple. A slender cat with eyes like polished emeralds lay on a warm stone and looked at him as if it could see through the veil between worlds. The priests told him that cats were guardians of the home, slayers of serpents, keepers of balance; to harm one was to invite the anger of the gods. Amset wrote it all down — and wanted to know more.

So he walked the city. He saw cats weaving through markets, merchants sharing fish, children stopping their games to stroke soft fur. In the granaries he watched them hunt with silent precision, protecting the harvest. In noble houses he saw them on cushions with golden collars; in temples, carved in stone with eyes of lapis lazuli.

One evening by the river an old woman told him a legend: Bastet once came down as a lioness to tear apart the chaos that threatened Ma'at, the order of the world. In time her fierce form softened into the cat — a reminder that even small beings can carry great power. "They are our bridge to the gods," she said. "Their eyes see what we cannot."

The cat and the ibis

The high priest had asked for a lesson, so Amset compared the cat with the ibis, sacred to Thoth, the god of wisdom and writing. Both were guardians: the ibis of knowledge, the cat of hearth and harvest. But the ibis flew high above the Nile, distant and lofty, while the cat curled up beside people in quiet companionship. That was the lesson Amset wrote: the divine is not only in the heavens, but also in the everyday — in the purring presence that shares our homes. The priest read it and nodded: "You have seen the truth."

From Bastet to Wen

Three thousand years later, a cat again became a symbol people gather around — this time on the internet. The Wen cat was born from a poem by Meow, split into a trillion pieces and airdropped to more than a million Solana wallets in January 2024. Like Bastet's cats, it is a guardian of something shared: a community and its stories. And like those cats, it lives close to people — in memes, art, toys and jokes, not only on charts.

Cat legends of the winter season

The original post closed with a collection of winter tales about cats. We keep a few of them — as folklore, which is exactly what they are:

  • Cats curled near the hearth were said to keep loneliness away on long winter nights.
  • In harsh winters, cats protected stored food from mice — the same job their Egyptian ancestors did.
  • Victorian Christmas cards often showed cats as symbols of comfort and good fortune.
  • Japan's beckoning cat, the maneki-neko, waves for luck in shops and homes all year round.
  • Every winter, shelters see cats find new homes — a reminder that the season is about kindness.
